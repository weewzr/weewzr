## Overview

This is the final report of a five-member team for the NUS course CN5303 Industrial Case Studies for Renewable Energy Systems (academic year 2026/27). It asks how far a grid-connected data centre in Singapore can reduce its annual electricity-related emissions by combining efficiency measures with low-carbon electricity supply, and at what cost. Its recommendations are addressed to data centre operators and to the regulators (IMDA and EMA) who set licence conditions.

The reference facility is hypothetical. It sits in the low-carbon data centre park that JTC is developing on Jurong Island and has 100 MW of rated IT capacity, an 85% electrical load factor, an annual power usage effectiveness (PUE) of 1.25 and 8,760 operating hours a year. That gives 744,600 MWh of IT energy and 930,750 MWh of facility electricity a year (report Table 1.3, p. 9). Grid electricity is taken at 0.402 tCO₂/MWh and an all-in S$200/MWh. The report is coursework, completed in November 2026 and presented on 12 November 2026. It has not been peer reviewed.

<Callout title="What kind of evidence this is" tone="limit">
The study is a desk-based techno-economic assessment. Its inputs are published research, agency and standards documents, vendor and cost literature, and stated design assumptions. The cooling results come from a calibrated simulation of an NUS testbed plant, published by other researchers, and are transferred to the reference facility by assumption. Nothing was built or measured at the reference facility, and the hourly matching score is labelled illustrative in the report.
</Callout>

## Abstract

The report concludes that Singapore can grow its data centre sector while cutting the sector's electricity-related emissions by about 90%. The route combines cooling optimisation with rooftop solar photovoltaics (PV), low-carbon ammonia generation and, in the longer term, small modular reactors (SMRs), because data centres already use more than a tenth of national electricity on a grid that is about 94% gas-fired.

Rooftop PV supplies only 1.6 GWh a year but at S$85/MWh, below the grid price. A 60 MWe direct-ammonia combined-cycle plant supplies 446.8 GWh a year at S$342/MWh and, with certified renewable fuel, emits 0.111 tCO₂e/MWh on a life-cycle basis, 72% below the grid. One 77 MWe SMR module supplies 627.3 GWh a year of firm power at S$230/MWh. Efficiency is applied first so that each source serves the reduced demand. Physics-informed predictive control of the chilled-water plant and SS 697 temperature optimisation save 17.0 GWh a year and bring PUE to 1.227, at a net saving of S$2.9 to 3.4 million a year. Hybrid liquid cooling then reaches PUE 1.20.

Six gaps in Singapore's present approach are turned into design requirements, and the measures are combined into six configurations. The integrated long-term portfolio cuts emissions to 37.0 ktCO₂e a year, avoiding about 337 ktCO₂e (90.1%) at S$145/tCO₂e. The ammonia price and its certified life-cycle intensity dominate the cost result (report p. 3).

## The engineering challenge

Singapore hosted about 70 data centres with a maximum IT load of around 1.6 GW by October 2026. They used about 6.5 TWh in 2024, more than a tenth of national consumption and up from about 7% in 2020, while natural gas supplied about 94% of generation at a grid emission factor of 0.402 kg CO₂/kWh. The national targets are net zero by 2050 and 45 to 50 MtCO₂e by 2035 (report p. 4).

The report names four constraints (Section 1.5, p. 11).

- **Low-carbon supply.** Singapore had 2.3 GWp of solar in October 2026 and targets 3 GWp by 2030 and 4 GWp by 2035, yet solar can meet only about a tenth of projected 2050 needs. Wind (average speeds of 2 to 3 m/s) and hydropower are ruled out in Section 2.2, p. 14.
- **Reliability.** The load is flat over 24 hours, so it needs firm generation with grid balancing and backup. Singapore plans around a 27% reserve margin.
- **Land and water.** Median water use of about 2.1 m³/MWh favours integrated sites such as the Jurong Island park.
- **Cooling climate.** Heat and humidity make cooling up to 40% of a typical Singapore data centre's energy, and free cooling is unavailable. AI racks can draw up to 50 times the power of a conventional rack.

The policy framework in Table 1.1 (p. 4) includes DC-CFA2 (PUE 1.25 or better at 100% IT load and at least 50% of new capacity on eligible green pathways), the SS 697:2023 tropical standard, SS 726:2026 for liquid cooling, and the Digital Infrastructure Bill passed on 7 October 2026. The reference facility alone equals about 1.6% of national consumption in 2025 (p. 10).

## Engineering analysis

**Annual demand and emissions (Section 1.4, pp. 7 to 8).**

E<sub>DC</sub> = P<sub>IT</sub> × LF × PUE × H

CO<sub>2</sub> = Σ<sub>i</sub> E<sub>i</sub> EF<sub>i</sub>, with Σ<sub>i</sub> E<sub>i</sub> = E<sub>DC</sub>

AC = ΔC / ΔCO<sub>2</sub>

E<sub>DC</sub> is facility electricity (MWh/yr), P<sub>IT</sub> rated IT capacity (MW), LF the load factor, H operating hours (h/yr), E<sub>i</sub> the electricity allocated from source i (MWh/yr) and EF<sub>i</sub> its emission factor (tCO₂e/MWh). ΔC is the change in annual cost (S$/yr), ΔCO<sub>2</sub> the emissions avoided (tCO₂e/yr) and AC the abatement cost (S$/tCO₂e). Grid power is priced at its delivered price and the generation pathways at levelised cost.

**Generation and levelised cost (Section 2.8, p. 24).**

E<sub>i</sub> = P<sub>i</sub> × CF<sub>i</sub> × H

LCOE = 1,000 × [CAPEX × CRF + FOM] / (H × CF) + VOM + Fuel

CAPEX is S$/kW, FOM S$/kW-yr, VOM and Fuel S$/MWh, and CRF the capital recovery factor. The report uses a 7% discount rate, 25 years for PV and ammonia and 40 years for the SMR.

**Ammonia emission factor (Section 2.5, pp. 21 to 22).**

EF<sub>NH3</sub> = SFC<sub>NH3</sub> × I<sub>NH3</sub> + EF<sub>N2O</sub>, with SFC<sub>NH3</sub> = 3.6 / (η<sub>NH3</sub> × LHV<sub>NH3</sub>)

SFC is fuel use (tNH₃/MWh), I<sub>NH3</sub> the delivered life-cycle intensity (tCO₂e/tNH₃), η the net efficiency and LHV the lower heating value (18.8 MJ/kg). The central intensity is 0.276 tCO₂e/tNH₃ (production 0.03, embodied renewable electricity 0.126, shipping 0.12), giving 0.111 tCO₂e/MWh.

**Cooling and PUE (Section 3.5, pp. 37 to 38).**

PUE = 1 + CSE + P<sub>other</sub> / P<sub>IT</sub>, with ΔE = E<sub>IT</sub> (PUE<sub>0</sub> − PUE<sub>1</sub>)

ΔE<sub>1+2</sub> = ΔE<sub>1</sub> + s × (4 − 1.7) × (E<sub>cool,0</sub> − ΔE<sub>1</sub>)

CSE is cooling-system efficiency, P<sub>cool</sub>/P<sub>IT</sub> (dimensionless). ΔE<sub>1</sub> is the plant-control saving, s the SS 697 saving per °C of temperature rise (2% central, 5% upper bound) and E<sub>cool,0</sub> the baseline cooling energy (MWh/yr). The second equation removes the overlap between plant control, which already raises supply water by 1.7 °C, and the 4 °C hall temperature rise of SS 697.

**Physics-informed model and control (Sections 3.3 and 3.4, pp. 30 to 34).** Published NUS work supplies the thermal model T<sub>DH</sub>(k+1) = T<sub>DH</sub>(k) − a[T<sub>DH</sub>(k) − T<sub>SA</sub>(k)]v<sub>SA</sub>(k) + b P<sub>ITE</sub>(k) + D<sub>k</sub>, with a and b constrained positive. T<sub>DH</sub> is data hall temperature (°C), T<sub>SA</sub> and v<sub>SA</sub> are supply-air temperature and flow, P<sub>ITE</sub> is IT power and D<sub>k</sub> a learned outdoor term. A predictive controller built on it resets chiller supply-water temperature and pump and fan speeds every 15 minutes within a 17 to 19 °C band. The report adopts these published results and does not rebuild the model.

**Allocation and decomposition (Section 5.2, p. 54).**

ΔCO<sub>2</sub> = E<sub>IT</sub> (PUE<sub>0</sub> − PUE<sub>1</sub>) EF<sub>grid</sub> + E<sub>IT</sub> PUE<sub>1</sub> (EF<sub>grid</sub> − EF<sub>eff</sub>)

The first term is the CO₂ removed by using less electricity and the second the CO₂ removed by cleaning the electricity still used, so no reduction is counted twice. EF<sub>eff</sub> is the effective emission factor of the proposal (tCO₂e/MWh).

**Hourly matching (Section 4.5, p. 48).**

CFE<sub>hourly</sub> = Σ<sub>h</sub> min(E<sub>CFE,h</sub>, E<sub>DC,h</sub>) / Σ<sub>h</sub> E<sub>DC,h</sub>

E<sub>CFE,h</sub> is eligible carbon-free electricity in hour h and E<sub>DC,h</sub> metered consumption in that hour (MWh). Supply above the hour's demand earns no credit.

**National scale (Section 3.6, p. 40).** Plant control alone is scaled to Singapore's 6.5 TWh at an assumed fleet PUE of 1.5. Full adoption would save 56 to 78 GWh a year, about 1% of the sector's 2.6 Mt CO₂.

**Recommended strategy** (Sections 5.6 and 5.7, pp. 60 to 61). The plan has five phases: cooling optimisation and rooftop PV (2026 to 2029), hybrid liquid cooling (2028 to 2032), contracting certified ammonia electricity (from about 2030), potential SMR supply (late 2030s onwards, subject to a national decision) and verified operation throughout. The report recommends that operators start with efficiency across the existing fleet.

## Limitations

The report states these limits itself.

- Hourly dispatch, transmission and market modelling are outside the assessment, and sensitivity is tested one variable at a time with no multivariable uncertainty model (pp. 6 to 7).
- The study covers electricity-related emissions, not a full Scope 1 to 3 inventory, and excludes financing and procurement (pp. 5 to 7).
- The cooling result is a calibrated simulation of the NUS testbed plant, not a field measurement, and its transfer to a commercial plant is an engineering assumption (pp. 7 and 32).
- Efficiency implementation cost is not published, so S$1 to 5 million is assumed, and hybrid liquid cooling gets only a break-even capital value (p. 40).
- Singapore has not decided to deploy nuclear energy, the late-2030s SMR date is the report's own assumption, and the ammonia plant awaits a final investment decision (pp. 7, 17 and 21).
- The 85% load factor, the PUE 1.20 target and the fleet PUE of 1.5 are assumptions, and the hourly score uses assumed availability and demand profiles (pp. 9, 38, 40 and 49).

As a desk study it has further limits that I add here. No input was independently checked, so the results are only as reliable as the cited sources, and the ammonia cost depends on a fuel price that has no long-term market yet.
